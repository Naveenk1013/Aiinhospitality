Service Robotics and Automation by Naveen Kumar

AI in Hospitality, Module 2. Tap, slide and play with each idea. Every robot is explained by the job it does for a guest or a team.

What is a service robot?

  • A machine that does useful work for people (not factory work), often near guests.
  • It senses (cameras, sensors), thinks (AI, maps) and acts (moves, speaks, carries).
  • Automation is the wider idea: any system that does a task with little human effort, with or without a robot (self check-in kiosk, conveyor).
  • Goal in hospitality: take over repetitive, physical or dangerous tasks so staff can spend time on warm, human service.
Real example: Henn na Hotel in Japan opened with robot staff at the front desk. Reports say it later scaled back some robots, because guests still needed human help for tricky requests. Lesson: robots work best when planned around real guest needs.

Where do robots fit?

Positions are illustrative teaching guides, not measurements.

Benefits

  • Works 24/7 without tiredness
  • Consistent quality and speed
  • Contactless, hygienic service
  • Frees staff for guest relationships

Challenges

  • High purchase and maintenance cost
  • Cannot handle unusual situations well
  • Guests may find it impersonal
  • Privacy, safety and job-impact concerns

Activity: robot, human or together?

Robots at the front office

  • Reception robots handle routine check-in and check-out: greet, scan ID, confirm the booking, take payment and issue a key.
  • Concierge robots answer common guest questions about the hotel and the city, in several languages, any time of day.
  • Both connect to the hotel's PMS (property management system) and use NLP to understand speech.
  • Goal: remove repetitive questions and paperwork so staff can handle special requests and personal service.

Reception robot: the dinosaur receptionists

  • Where: Henn na Hotel at Huis Ten Bosch, Nagasaki, Japan. Opened in 2015 and recognised by Guinness as the world's first robot-staffed hotel.
  • What they looked like: talking robot dinosaurs (velociraptors) plus a humanoid receptionist, speaking Japanese, English, Korean and Chinese.
  • How check-in worked: guests used a touch panel and the robot guided them through.
  • What went wrong: the dinosaurs could not copy passports, so a human had to step in. Reports said the robots created more work for staff. The hotel cut its robots from 243 to about 85 by January 2019.

Concierge robot: Connie

  • Where: a pilot at the Hilton McLean in Virginia, USA, announced in March 2016.
  • Who built it: a NAO humanoid robot (Aldebaran), about 58 cm (23 in) tall, with IBM Watson speech and language services and WayBlazer travel knowledge. Named after Conrad Hilton.
  • What it does: answers questions about hotel services, restaurants and attractions. It cannot check guests in.
  • Smart part: it learns from guest conversations to improve its recommendations. IBM said it was meant to help concierges, not replace them.

    What the real hotels teach us

    • Robots are good at: repeatable questions, multiple languages, waiting at the desk at any hour, and drawing guest attention.
    • Robots are weak at: unusual requests, paperwork like copying passports, noisy lobbies, and showing empathy.
    • Best practice: a robot front line with a trained human always close by, and clear rules for when the robot hands over.
    • Guest feedback at Henn na: reviewers called it gimmicky and said it needed more human warmth. Technology works when it supports hospitality, not when it replaces it.

    Sources: Matador Network, NPR, The Points Guy, Blooloop, Time, Computerworld, InformationWeek, The Robot Report. These are media reports, not independent tests.

    Cleaning robots

    • Vacuum and mopping: map the floor and clean in lines.
    • UV disinfection: light that kills germs in empty rooms.
    • Window and pool cleaners for hard-to-reach areas.
    • Sensors avoid furniture and drops (stairs).

    Linen delivery robots

    • Carry towels, linen and amenities between laundry and floors.
    • Ride the lift by connecting to it over Wi-Fi.
    • Locked compartment opens for the right room or staff member.
    • Great for night deliveries.
    Example: Relay (see the 3D delivery below).

    Meet Whiz, the carpet-cleaning robot

    • Name: Whiz, an autonomous commercial vacuum made by SoftBank Robotics.
    • Job: vacuums carpets in corridors, lobbies and event spaces, so housekeepers can do detailed work like dusting, linen changes and bathrooms.
    • How it learns: a staff member drives it once along the route (teach), then it repeats that route on its own and steers around obstacles.
    • Memory and range: the distributor reports it can store up to 600 routes and vacuum about 16,000 sq ft on one charge.
    • Smart reporting: cloud software (Whiz Connect, on the BrainOS platform) shows how often it ran and how much floor it covered.

    Where is Whiz used in real hotels?

    Sheraton Heathrow, London, UK

    • A 4-star hotel with long corridors (about 1.6 km to clean).
    • Chosen because it is quiet enough for daytime cleaning while guests sleep.
    • Management reported less dust in corridors and used Whiz's reports to see how much area it covered.

    Pullman Paris Montparnasse, France

    • Flagship 4-star hotel using Whiz for corridors and event spaces.
    • The hotel reported 855 hours of use and 268,000 square metres vacuumed.
    • Staff time moved to guest-focused tasks.

    Hilton Garden Inn, Gilroy, California, USA

    • Whiz took over 10+ hours a week of repetitive vacuuming.
    • The GM reported guest cleanliness scores rising from 82 to 90 percent.

    Also reported: Garden City Hotel, Long Island, New York. Figures come from the hotels, SoftBank Robotics and RobotLAB, so they are vendor-reported results, not independent studies.

    Meet Relay, the room-delivery robot

    • Name: Relay, built by Savioke (the company is now called Relay Robotics). Hotels often give their own robot a nickname, such as Botlr.
    • Size: about 92 cm (3 ft) tall, roughly 45 kg (100 lb), with a lockable compartment under a lid.
    • Job: carries towels, linen, toiletries and snacks to guest rooms so staff do not have to leave their posts.
    • How it finds its way: a map of the hotel, Wi-Fi, 3D cameras and sensors to avoid people and obstacles.
    • Lift trick: it calls and rides the lift on its own using wireless communication.
    • Guest handover: it calls the room phone, and the lid opens only when the guest opens the door. Then it asks for a star rating and heads back to base.
    Real lesson: one hotel reported a guest dropping used, wet towels into the robot's compartment. Nobody had designed for that. Good service robots are tested around real guest behaviour.

      Robots in food and beverage

      • Robot waiters carry dishes from kitchen to table and back.
      • Automated kitchens use robot arms for repeatable tasks like frying.
      • Bartending robots measure and mix drinks exactly, every time.
      • Goal: remove long walks, heat and repetition so staff can focus on greeting, advising and hospitality.

      1. Robot waiter: meet BellaBot

      • Name: BellaBot, made by Pudu Robotics. A cat-faced robot with four trays in its slim body.
      • How it works: staff load plates and choose the table number on its screen. It drives there on a mapped route, speaks, and rotates its trays to the side so the guest can take the dish.
      • Smart touches: tray sensors and a light strip show when a dish is taken, it serves 3 to 4 tables per trip, and it has a birthday mode that sings and delivers cakes.
      • Best use: restaurants where the kitchen is far from the tables, or to remove dish-carrying so staff can talk to guests.

        2. Automated kitchen: meet Flippy 2

        • Name: Flippy 2, a robotic fry station from Miso Robotics.
        • How it works: a robot arm slides on an overhead rail, fills, lowers, lifts and empties fry baskets. Thermal-imaging cameras and AI vision identify the food in the basket.
        • What it does: takes over a whole fry station, so staff avoid the hottest, most repetitive job. The maker says it can handle about 60 baskets an hour.
        • Human role: staff still manage orders, seasoning, packing and quality.

          3. Bartending robot: meet the Bionic Bar

          • Name: Bionic Bar, built by Makr Shakr (Italy). Two robotic arms, with 30 or more spirits and mixers hanging upside down overhead.
          • How it works: the guest orders on a tablet, often swiping a room key. One arm measures, shakes, stirs or muddles. The other pours and serves.
          • Style: the arms' movements were modelled on a dancer, so watching them is part of the show.
          • Speed: early versions were described as making about 120 drinks an hour; a newer ship version is reported at up to 250.
          • Human role: live bartenders still work elsewhere on board. The robot is both a guest experience and a way to make drinks consistently.

            Robotics and automation in travel

            • Smart airports use biometrics, kiosks, AI cameras and robots to move passengers with fewer queues and fewer paper checks.
            • Autonomous baggage systems tag every bag, track it, and move it with machines instead of people lifting and sorting.
            • Why hospitality students care: airports, airlines, hotels and cruise lines share the same guest journey. Smooth travel starts the guest experience before arrival.

            1. Smart airport: meet Digi Yatra

            • Name: Digi Yatra, India's facial-recognition system for paperless, contactless airport entry.
            • How it works: the passenger enrols on the app (ID check, selfie, boarding pass). At the entry e-gate, security area and boarding gate, a camera matches the face to the booking and opens the gate.
            • Voluntary: passengers can still use the normal process. Privacy and consent are a big part of this topic.
            • Benefit shown in the Delhi trial: entering the terminal took about 5 seconds instead of up to 20.
            • Other smart airport tools: self check-in kiosks, self bag drop, queue-length cameras, guide robots and cleaning robots.

              2. Autonomous baggage: meet FLEET

              • Name: FLEET, an autonomous baggage vehicle system from Vanderlande (Netherlands).
              • How it works: each small vehicle carries one bag and chooses the best route. It replaces fixed conveyors and sorters.
              • Why airports like it: flexible if something breaks, easy to grow, and the maker says it can use up to 50 percent less energy than traditional systems.
              • Tags: every bag has a barcode or RFID tag so the system always knows where it is and which flight it needs.
